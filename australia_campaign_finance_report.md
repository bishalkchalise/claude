## 1) Executive summary
Australia has a **federal, multi-level political finance system**. There is no single “Australian campaign finance code”. Instead, obligations vary by election type and jurisdiction. Core regulatory levers are:
- disclosure/reporting of donations and electoral expenditure
- caps on donations and/or expenditure (in some jurisdictions)
- source restrictions (especially foreign donations; and sectoral bans in some states)
- public funding mechanisms
- compliance/audit/enforcement powers.

As of this review date, a major national reform package (Electoral Reform Act 2025) is implementing significant Commonwealth changes from **1 July 2026**, including lower disclosure thresholds and faster disclosure in election periods.

## 2) Constitutional and institutional context
- **Commonwealth:** regulated primarily under the *Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918* (Part XX and related provisions), administered by the Australian Electoral Commission (AEC).
- **States/territories:** each has separate electoral finance statutes and regulators.
- **Local government:** usually regulated by **state/territory law**, either in the same funding/disclosure statute used for state elections or via dedicated local-government election legislation.

## 3) Commonwealth (federal) rules
### 3.1 Core law and administrator
- *Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918*.
- AEC Funding and Disclosure framework.

### 3.2 Key architecture
Historically, Commonwealth law has emphasized disclosure and public funding, with tighter controls added progressively (including foreign-donation controls and regulation of third-party political campaigners).

### 3.3 Current reform phase (critical)
AEC guidance states the *Electoral Legislation Amendment (Electoral Reform) Act 2025* is a major overhaul, with changes commencing from **1 July 2026**, including:
- lower disclosure threshold (AEC fact sheets indicate $5,000, indexed after general elections)
- expedited/near-real-time donation disclosure during election periods
- revised annual/election return arrangements
- revised publication and compliance settings.

Because transitional arrangements and subordinate instruments matter, users should verify operative dates and instruments on the Federal Register and AEC reform pages before relying on a specific obligation.

## 4) State and territory frameworks
> Important: rules differ substantially, and some thresholds/caps are indexed or changed by periodic notices.

### New South Wales (NSW)
- Primary law: *Electoral Funding Act 2018 (NSW)*.
- Features: donation caps, expenditure caps, disclosure framework, and application to state and local election participants under NSW’s scheme.
- Regulator: NSW Electoral Commission.

### Victoria (VIC)
- Primary law: *Electoral Act 2002 (Vic)* (Part 12 and related amendments).
- Features: caps/disclosure framework for state elections; foreign-donation restrictions; anti-avoidance provisions.
- Local-council donation regulation is administered separately (via local government integrity architecture), even though state-level rules are in the Electoral Act.
- Regulator: Victorian Electoral Commission (state-level disclosures).

### Queensland (QLD)
- Primary laws: *Electoral Act 1992 (Qld)* and *Local Government Electoral Act 2011 (Qld)*.
- Features: disclosure regime for political donations/expenditure, donation caps, expenditure caps, and restrictions affecting certain donor classes.
- Regulator: Electoral Commission of Queensland.

### Western Australia (WA)
- Primary law: *Electoral Act 1907 (WA)* (as amended, including 2023 reforms) plus regulations.
- Features: enhanced disclosure and political finance controls from July 2024 reforms, including foreign donation settings and spending/disclosure controls.
- Regulator: WA Electoral Commission.
- Local-government gift rules also operate under WA local-government election framework.

### South Australia (SA)
- Primary law: *Electoral Act 1985 (SA)* (notably amended by the *Electoral (Accountability and Integrity) Amendment Act 2024*).
- Features: SA has moved to one of the strongest interventionist models, with broad donation prohibitions and tightly structured exceptions/controls (including treatment of new entrants and third parties under caps/reporting rules).
- Regulator: Electoral Commission of South Australia.

### Tasmania (TAS)
- Primary law: *Electoral Disclosure and Funding Act 2023 (Tas)*.
- Features: modernized disclosure/funding framework for Tasmanian parliamentary elections.
- Regulator: Tasmanian Electoral Commission.

### Australian Capital Territory (ACT)
- Primary law: *Electoral Act 1992 (ACT)*.
- Features: disclosure obligations, donation and expenditure controls, and explicit treatment of third-party campaigners.
- Regulator: Elections ACT.

### Northern Territory (NT)
- Primary law: *Electoral Act 2004 (NT)*.
- Features: Part 10 framework for donations, expenditure and disclosure; reporting obligations for parties/candidates/donors and election-period controls.
- Regulator: Northern Territory Electoral Commission.

## 5) Local-government level
There is no uniform national local-government campaign finance code. In practice:
- NSW and QLD: local election participants are directly integrated into state electoral finance/disclosure systems.
- WA and others: local-government-specific gift/disclosure rules coexist with broader state electoral finance law.
- Victoria: local-council candidate donations involve separate administration pathways from state election disclosures.

**Practical implication:** compliance must be mapped election-by-election (state lower house, upper house, council, mayoral, by-election), because definitions of “gift”, “political donation”, aggregation, return timing, and enforcement differ.

## 6) Major reforms over time (high-level timeline)
- **1980s (Commonwealth):** public funding and modern disclosure architecture introduced.
- **2000s (Commonwealth):** higher disclosure thresholds with indexation became a central controversy.
- **2018 (Commonwealth):** *Electoral Legislation Amendment (Electoral Funding and Disclosure Reform) Act 2018* tightened foreign-donation and third-party campaigner regulation.
- **2023–2025 (states):** substantial state-level redesigns (e.g., WA amendments effective 2024; SA’s major 2024 amending Act effective from 2025 settings).
- **2025–2026 (Commonwealth):** *Electoral Reform Act 2025* rollout from 1 July 2026, including lower threshold and faster disclosure.

## 7) Key policy debates
1. **Transparency timing:** annual disclosure vs near-real-time reporting.
2. **Donation caps vs free political communication:** constitutional proportionality and implied freedom issues.
3. **Third-party regulation:** balancing anti-circumvention with civil society participation.
4. **Foreign influence controls:** broad consensus on restrictions, disagreement on scope and enforcement burden.
5. **Public funding design:** anti-corruption and fairness benefits vs incumbent advantage concerns.
6. **National consistency vs federal diversity:** harmonisation could reduce compliance burden but may reduce state experimentation.
7. **Local government vulnerability:** planning/development decision interfaces drive stronger calls for tighter council-level donation controls.

## 8) Academic/policy research themes
Australian scholarship and policy analysis consistently examine:
- whether high thresholds and long lags reduce practical transparency
- how caps and bans alter party revenue models and campaign strategies
- constitutional litigation risk in design of caps/exemptions
- whether reforms reduce “dark money” and regulatory arbitrage across jurisdictions
- local-government integrity risks around property development and donor influence.

Useful recurring sources include Parliamentary Library quick guides, JSCEM inquiries/reviews, integrity body reports (e.g., anti-corruption inquiries at state level), and election regulator post-election disclosure/compliance reports.

## 9) Practical compliance checklist (for campaigns/advocacy groups)
1. Identify election type and jurisdiction first.
2. Determine whether you are a party, candidate, associated entity, third-party campaigner, donor, or publisher/broadcaster.
3. Check current indexed thresholds/caps for the relevant financial year.
4. Confirm whether “federal purpose” rules are triggered for mixed campaigns.
5. Maintain contemporaneous donation source verification and expenditure records.
6. Track annual, election-period, and expedited return deadlines separately.
7. Reconcile local-government-specific rules where applicable.
8. Obtain legal advice for cross-jurisdiction campaigns.
